Visual Aids: Curing "Death by PowerPoint"

Designing slides that enhance, not replace, your message for a truly professional impact.

🧠 Cognitive Load Theory

The human brain has two separate channels for processing information: auditory and visual. However, they share a limited amount of working memory. When you display a slide full of text while speaking, the audience's brain experiences "cognitive overload." They cannot read and listen at the same time efficiently—usually, they choose to read your slide and stop listening to you entirely.

1. The "Rule of One"

A slide is not a teleprompter; it's an emotional and visual anchor. Every slide you show should communicate exactly one idea. If you have three points to make, use three slides. This keeps the audience's focus moving at your pace.

Fig 1: Slides should be "glance-able." The audience should understand the slide in 3 seconds and return their gaze to you.
Do: Use High-Quality Images

Use a single, powerful image that evokes the emotion of your point. A picture of a growing plant is more memorable than a bullet point saying "Our company is growing."

Don't: Read Your Slides

If the audience can read your entire speech on the screen, you are redundant. Your slides should provide the "What," but you must provide the "How" and "Why."

2. Visual Hierarchy and Eye Tracking

The audience's eyes naturally follow a path on the screen. If you place a bright red chart in the corner, they will look there first. You must design your slides to guide their eyes toward the most important information at the exact moment you mention it.

Fig 2: Use contrast and size to dictate what the audience looks at first. Control the journey.

3. The 10/20/30 Rule

A famous principle by Guy Kawasaki for high-stakes presentations: Try to have no more than 10 slides, keep the presentation under 20 minutes, and use a font size of at least 30 points. If you need a smaller font, it means you have too much text.

🎬 The "Anti-Death" Checklist:
  • The Squint Test: Squint your eyes at your slide. If you can't tell what the main focus is, the slide is too complex.
  • Contrast is King: Use dark text on a light background or vice versa. Avoid low-contrast colors like yellow text on a white background.
  • Black Out: Don't be afraid to use a completely black slide when you want the audience to focus 100% on a story you are telling.
  • Animation Caution: Use animations to reveal information step-by-step, but avoid "spinning" or "bouncing" transitions—they look unprofessional.

"Slides are like a movie soundtrack: if they are perfect, you barely notice them, but you feel their impact."
